Matter and Measurement

Before you can do chemistry, you need two things: a clear way to describe what matter is, and a reliable way to measure it. This page covers both — classification, states, properties, units, significant figures, conversions, and density.

Classification of Matter

  • Element: a pure substance made of one kind of atom (e.g., Fe, O2). It cannot be broken down by chemical means.
  • Compound: a pure substance of two or more elements chemically combined in a fixed ratio (e.g., H2O, NaCl). It can only be broken down by a chemical change.
  • Homogeneous mixture: uniform throughout — one phase (e.g., salt water, air). Composition can vary.
  • Heterogeneous mixture: not uniform — two or more phases you can see or separate physically (e.g., sand in water, oil and vinegar).

Separation follows the classification: mixtures separate by physical means (filtration, distillation, evaporation); compounds only by chemical means.

States of Matter

Solid: fixed shape and volume, particles locked in place. Liquid: fixed volume, takes the shape of its container, particles flow past each other. Gas: neither fixed shape nor fixed volume — it fills its container. (A fourth state, plasma, is ionized gas: lightning, the Sun.) Adding heat generally drives solid → liquid → gas; removing heat reverses it.

Physical vs Chemical Properties and Changes

A physical property is observed without changing what the substance is: colour, density, melting point, solubility. A chemical property describes how it reacts: flammability, acidity, rusting. Likewise, a physical change (melting, dissolving, cutting) changes form but not identity, while a chemical change (burning, rusting, cooking) makes new substances. Signs of a chemical change: colour change, gas produced, precipitate formed, heat or light released, or a new smell.

SI Units and Prefixes

The base SI units you will use most: length in metres (m), mass in kilograms (kg), time in seconds (s), temperature in kelvin (K), amount in moles (mol). Common prefixes: kilo- ($10^3$), centi- ($10^{-2}$), milli- ($10^{-3}$), micro- ($10^{-6}$), nano- ($10^{-9}$). Temperature: $K = °C + 273.15$; note that a change of 1 °C equals a change of 1 K.

Significant Figures

Significant figures (sig figs) record how precisely a measurement was made. The rules:

  • All nonzero digits count: 42.7 has 3 sig figs.
  • Zeros between nonzero digits count: 1004 has 4.
  • Leading zeros never count: 0.00457 has 3 (4, 5, 7).
  • Trailing zeros count only if a decimal point is present: 250.0 has 4, but 250 has 2 (unless written as $2.50 \times 10^2$).
  • Exact numbers (counted items, defined conversions like 1 km = 1000 m) have unlimited sig figs.

Rounding: if the first digit dropped is 5 or more, round the last kept digit up; if it is 4 or less, leave it. In multiplication and division, the answer has as many sig figs as the least precise measurement; in addition and subtraction, as many decimal places as the least precise measurement.

Example: Round 12.3456 to four significant figures. The first four sig figs are 1, 2, 3, 4, and the next digit is 5, so round the 4 up: $$12.35$$ And 0.0045678 to three significant figures is 0.00457 — the digits kept are 4, 5, 6, and the next digit (7) rounds the 6 up to 7.

Dimensional Analysis

Convert units by multiplying by conversion factors — fractions equal to 1, arranged so unwanted units cancel: $$\text{starting quantity} \times \frac{\text{new unit}}{\text{old unit}}$$ Chain factors for multi-step conversions, and always check that the final unit is the one you want.

Example: Convert 90.0 km/h to m/s: $$90.0\ \frac{\text{km}}{\text{h}} \times \frac{1000\ \text{m}}{1\ \text{km}} \times \frac{1\ \text{h}}{3600\ \text{s}} = \frac{90{,}000}{3600}\ \frac{\text{m}}{\text{s}} = 25.0\ \text{m/s}$$ The km and h cancel, leaving m/s. Three sig figs in, three sig figs out.

Density

Density links mass and volume: $$d = \frac{m}{V}$$ It is an intensive property — it does not depend on how much you have. Common units are g/mL (or g/cm3) for solids and liquids. An object floats in a liquid if its density is lower than the liquid's.

Example: A sample has mass 45.0 g and volume 15.0 mL: $$d = \frac{45.0\ \text{g}}{15.0\ \text{mL}} = 3.00\ \text{g/mL}$$

Practice Problems

  1. Classify each as element, compound, homogeneous mixture, or heterogeneous mixture: (a) distilled water, (b) air, (c) iron filings, (d) salt water.
  2. A metal block has mass 125 g and volume 25.0 cm3. Calculate its density.
  3. Convert 72.0 km/h to m/s.
  4. How many significant figures are in 0.005040? Round 8.4567 to three significant figures.

Answers: 1. (a) compound (b) homogeneous mixture (c) element (d) homogeneous mixture   2. 5.00 g/cm3   3. 20.0 m/s   4. 4 sig figs; 8.46
